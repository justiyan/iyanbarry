A team cannot plan reliably if every improvement is scheduled into the time left after support work. Some weeks there may be room. Other weeks the same plan becomes an expectation that people will somehow finish it after everything else.

The answer is not to pretend operational demand is predictable. It is to make the uncertainty visible and agree what happens when demand is heavier than expected.

I would protect a small amount of improvement work, define when it can be interrupted and give sponsors an honest account of what can be promised. That is a planning choice, not a claim that the team has discovered extra hours.

## Measure the demand that competes with improvement

Look beyond ticket counts. Include incident handling, routine maintenance, supplier coordination, access work and the informal requests that arrive through messages or conversations.

Use a practical level of recording. The purpose is to understand demand and interruption, not to require staff to account for every minute. Group work sufficiently to see which activities vary and which commitments recur.

Review a representative period, including busy weeks if records allow. An average can conceal the weeks in which project time disappears. Note unusual events and avoid treating a short quiet period as evidence of permanent spare capacity.

Ask who can perform each type of work. A team may appear to have capacity overall while the one person needed for an improvement is continually interrupted. Skills and access constraints belong in the plan alongside the total workload.

## Offer a range with conditions attached

Once demand is visible, distinguish a minimum commitment from additional work that depends on conditions. Make both useful and small enough to assess.

For a fictional team, the minimum might be completing a defined access-process review during the next planning period. If incident demand remains manageable, the team may also implement the agreed change. These are illustrative commitments, not measured delivery promises.

Explain what "manageable" means using the team's evidence and service obligations. A numerical capacity range should come from actual records or clearly labelled estimates, not a standard percentage borrowed from another organisation.

Attach assumptions to the range: planned leave, available specialists, known releases and the interruption rule. When an assumption changes, update the commitment promptly. Do not wait until the end of the period to reveal that the lower end was the only plausible outcome.

Avoid allowing stakeholders to treat the upper end as the promise. The minimum is the commitment the team expects to protect under the stated conditions. The additional work is conditional and should not be scheduled as a dependency for another project without discussion.

## Protect time through a decision rule

Put the improvement work into the same visible plan as operational work. Give it an owner, a defined result and protected working periods where practical.

Then agree what can interrupt it. A major service incident may justify interruption. A routine request should normally follow the established queue unless an authorised person changes the priority with full knowledge of the displaced work.

Name who can make that call. Staff should not have to negotiate separately with every requester while also trying to finish the task. The leader needs to support the rule when an influential person asks for an exception.

[Leading a team out of firefighting](/blog/leading-it-teams-in-the-real-world) depends on this distinction between real operational urgency and work that is simply arriving loudly. The rule will need judgement, but that judgement should have a consistent home.

Record interruptions and the commitments they displaced. Use the record to improve planning, not to blame the person who responded to a genuine incident.

## Choose improvements that fit the available attention

A large programme may be a poor first choice for a team with variable operational demand. Look for a bounded change that removes a recurring source of work and can be completed safely in manageable stages.

In a fictional example, repeated access queries may justify clarifying the approval route and publishing a checked request form before attempting broad automation. The smaller change should solve a real part of the problem, not merely create a document that nobody uses.

Agree how the result will be assessed. Did the queries change? Are requests reaching the right approver? Has effort moved elsewhere? Do not assume completion means capacity has been released.

Be careful about promising to fund the next improvement with anticipated time savings. First verify that the burden reduced and that the relevant person can use the released time. A few scattered minutes are not automatically equivalent to an uninterrupted engineering session.

## Replan without treating exhaustion as contingency

When operational demand exceeds the plan, name the consequence. Reduce scope, move a commitment or obtain appropriately approved help. Do not quietly make longer hours the default response.

Safe Work Australia identifies job demands, low job control and lack of role clarity among common psychosocial hazards. That is a reason to take the design of work seriously, not a diagnosis of any particular team or a substitute for jurisdiction-specific workplace advice.

Review repeated overruns with the team. They may indicate an unrealistic service promise, a recurring fault or a capacity gap that small improvements cannot solve alone. Put that evidence into the [budget conversation](/blog/making-the-technology-budget-easier-to-challenge) rather than promising the same output again.

End each planning review by naming what will not be done. Tell the affected requester and preserve a route to reconsider the choice. A protected improvement commitment becomes credible when everyone understands which competing work must wait and when that decision is allowed to change.
