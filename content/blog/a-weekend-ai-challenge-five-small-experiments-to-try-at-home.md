A lot of people have tried AI once or twice, got a generic answer and quietly moved on. That's understandable. It usually gives you a generic answer when you ask it a generic question.

A weekend is a good time to try it with lower stakes. These five experiments are useful on their own, and each one practises a habit that makes AI more useful at work.

Use whichever assistant you already have access to. Some let you control whether your conversations are used to improve the service, so check those settings before you start, especially on a personal account.

## 1. Make dinner from what's already in the fridge

Take a photo of the fridge or pantry, or type a quick list. Then ask for three dinner ideas.

Add the details that matter: how much time you have, who's eating, what they won't eat and what equipment you want to use. "Something easy for dinner" and "something ready in 30 minutes, using one pan, for two adults and a nine-year-old who won't eat mushrooms" are very different requests.

Treat the result as a starting point. Double-check anything to do with allergies or food safety yourself.

*The work habit: give it enough context to understand the actual problem.*

## 2. Plan Saturday around real life

Ask for a Saturday plan, then give it your constraints. There might be sport at 9am, rain in the afternoon, a budget of $80 and a dog that needs a walk.

Once you get the first plan, push back. Ask it to make the day less rushed, keep the driving under an hour, or give you an indoor backup.

If it suggests a place, check the opening hours and details directly before you go.

*The work habit: treat the first answer as a draft and improve it with feedback.*

## 3. Get help understanding something confusing

Choose something you've been meaning to understand, such as an insurance renewal, a car service quote or a school permission note. Remove names, account numbers and anything private first.

Ask it to explain the document in plain English. Then ask which questions you should raise before making a decision.

This works best when AI helps you think through the information. It shouldn't replace proper advice when the decision has financial, legal or medical consequences.

*The work habit: use AI to understand the issue, then check it before you decide.*

## 4. Settle a friendly argument properly

Pick something low stakes, like whether a movie is really a Christmas film, or why a particular sport has a strange rule.

Ask for the strongest case on each side. If it gives factual claims, ask where they come from and open the sources yourself.

You may find the confident answer is less settled than it first sounded.

*The work habit: check the evidence behind a confident answer.*

## 5. Break down the job you've been avoiding

Maybe it's the garage, the spare room or years of photos on your phone. Describe the job, how much time you have and what usually makes you put it off.

Ask it to turn the job into 20-minute tasks, with the easiest one first. Then do one task.

It won't clean the garage for you, but a smaller first step is often enough to get started.

*The work habit: break a large problem into steps someone can actually do.*

## What carries over to work

You don't need a special technical skill to get better results. Most of it comes back to four habits:

- Explain the situation and what matters.
- Set clear limits.
- Keep improving the result instead of accepting the first answer.
- Check anything important before you rely on it.

The same care about information applies too. Don't put customer, employee or confidential business information into a tool unless your organisation has approved it for that purpose. If you're thinking about what that looks like for a team, [using AI as an IT leader](/blog/using-ai-as-an-it-leader) covers some of the practical side.

Give one experiment a go this weekend. It's a relaxed way to learn how AI works in practice, and you might even get a good dinner out of it.
